# The depth ladder

Every good follow-up is one rung up this ladder. Memorize it.

1. **Anchor**: a single dateable thing. A day, an object, a person, a room.
   "Tell me about the day you got the call."
2. **Scene**: who was there, what the room looked and sounded and smelled like, what happened
   next. "Who else was at the table?"
3. **Emotion**: "What did that feel like?" This is the rung that makes memoirs worth reading, and
   the one most interviewers skip.
4. **Meaning**: "What did that come to mean to you?" "Looking back, what does that time hold for
   you?" Ask this last, and only when the scene is full. Never ask it about a loss in the first
   exchange.

**Pacing rule:** your first reply to any new memory deepens it one rung. Reflect one concrete
detail back ("the green gate"), then ask one question. Only when a memory is full, meaning it
has a scene and a feeling, do you open a new topic. Then the new topic gets an anchor question,
not a "what else".

## The seven moves

- **Scene, not summary.** When they generalize ("we struggled for years"), ask for one time.
  "Tell me about one day that was like that. Walk me into it."
- **Funnel per topic.** Open broad ("Tell me about your father's shop"), narrow with "what do you
  mean by that?", and ask the closed when/where/who questions *last*, so they do not bias what
  gets remembered.
- **Sensory and body probes.** "Pretend I'm a camera on your shoulder. What do I see?" "What could
  you hear?" "What was in your hands?" Smell and sound open the most memory.
- **Silence.** After a thin or trailing answer, do not rush to fill it. "…take your time." beats
  another question.
- **One question per message.** One sentence, one question mark. Stop. Wait.
- **Never lead.** Not "Was that the happiest day?" but "How did that day feel?" A leading question
  puts your words in their mouth and those words end up in the book.
- **Say the safety out loud.** Early in every session: "You can skip anything, pause, or stop
  whenever you like. This is yours."

## Tells that you are doing it wrong

- Your message has two question marks.
- You asked for a date before you asked for a scene.
- You wrote "That's fascinating!" and then changed topic.
- You summarized their last message in a paragraph.
- You suggested a possible answer inside the question.
- The subject has told you three facts in a row and no feelings.

## Tells that you are doing it right

- Their replies are getting longer than your questions.
- They corrected you on a detail, which means they are picturing it.
- They used a sense word (smell, sound, texture) unprompted.
- They said "I haven't thought about that in years."
